WordPiece Tokenizer, Step by Step

Every word starts split into single characters. One merge at a time, the algorithm combines whichever adjacent pair of pieces scores highest — until common word fragments like ##ing and un become tokens of their own. This is the subword algorithm BERT uses. Step through it, or press Play.

Trained from scratch, in your browser, on a ~55-word toy corpus — real WordPiece vocabularies are trained on billions of words and stopped early at a target size (often 30,000 tokens) specifically so they still generalize to words they've never seen. This corpus is small enough to run all the way to the end, where every word becomes a single token — which is the point where a real tokenizer would already have stopped.

How to read this

  1. A "piece" is a symbol — a single character at first, later a merged chunk. A piece that continues a word (rather than starting one) is marked with a leading ##, e.g. ##ing — that marker is real, literal token text a WordPiece tokenizer actually emits, not decoration.
  2. The score isn't just frequency. score = freq(pair) / (freq(left) × freq(right)) — dividing by how common each piece already is on its own means two rare pieces that almost only ever appear together can outrank two very common pieces that simply collide a lot. That normalization is WordPiece's actual difference from plain BPE, which would just merge the single most frequent pair.
  3. Merging replaces every occurrence of the winning pair, in every word, with one new symbol — added to the vocabulary for good.
  4. Tokenizing new text (the words never in training, and anything you type below) is a separate step: greedy longest-match, left to right. At each position it looks for the longest piece in the vocabulary and takes it. If no piece at all matches — not even a single character — the whole word becomes [UNK]. WordPiece doesn't partially cover a word; one uncovered character sinks the entire thing.
